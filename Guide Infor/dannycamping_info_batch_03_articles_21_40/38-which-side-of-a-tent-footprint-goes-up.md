# Which Side of a Tent Footprint Goes Up?

There is no universal rule that every tent footprint has a “shiny side up” or “coated side down.”

The correct orientation depends on **how the footprint was designed and coated**. Some manufacturers explicitly instruct users to put the coated side up. Others make footprints where attachment hardware and shape make orientation obvious.

Use model-specific instructions whenever they exist.

## Start With the Attachment Points

A model-specific footprint may have color-coded corners, grommets, buckles, hooks, printed logos, or different front and rear geometry.

Lay it under the tent and match each corner.

If one orientation aligns naturally with pole feet or buckles, that is your strongest clue.

## Check the Manufacturer Instructions

Search the manual for “footprint setup,” “fast pitch,” “ground cloth,” or “coated side.”

One REI footprint instruction specifically tells users to place the coated side up.

That does not prove every footprint from every brand uses the same orientation.

## What If the Footprint Has a Shiny Side?

A shiny surface may indicate a coating, but appearance alone is not reliable.

Fabrics can have different finishes for manufacturing or abrasion reasons.

If you cannot identify the model, look for sewn labels, hardware orientation, or online instructions.

## What If Both Sides Look the Same?

Some materials are effectively symmetrical.

If there is no different coating, printing, or hardware orientation, the more important factors are correct shape, size, and keeping edges beneath the floor.

## Why Some Coated Sides Face Up

A coating can be protected from direct ground abrasion when it faces the tent floor, while the tougher face contacts soil.

But designs differ.

The footprint may also serve as part of a fly-first setup where hardware must face a particular way.

## How to Set Up a Footprint Correctly

1. Choose durable, well-drained ground.
2. Clear loose sharp debris.
3. Lay the footprint flat.
4. Align front and rear.
5. Match corner attachments.
6. Pitch the tent over it.
7. Confirm edges do not protrude.

The last step matters more for rain management than which decorative side is visible.

## Why the Footprint Should Not Stick Out

Exposed material catches rain.

Water can run beneath the tent floor.

A correctly sized footprint is usually slightly smaller than the floor or shaped so edges stay under it.

If using generic material, fold it in.

## Can You Cut a Generic Footprint?

Yes.

Measure the tent floor and cut the material slightly smaller.

Round corners to reduce exposed edges.

If using material that frays, finish the edge appropriately.

## Footprint on a Tent Platform

Orientation still follows the product design.

A footprint helps protect from rough timber.

Check for protruding screws or splinters before pitching.

Use approved platform anchors.

## Footprint on Wet Ground

The footprint reduces dirt and abrasion but is not a substitute for a waterproof floor.

If water is standing around the tent, improve site selection.

Do not rely on footprint orientation to solve drainage.

## Footprint for Fly-First Pitching

Some tents allow poles and fly to be pitched with the footprint while the inner tent is omitted or added later.

In this configuration, the footprint attachment points become structural.

Correct orientation is essential.

Follow the manual.

## What If You Bought a Used Footprint?

Identify brand, model, size, and corner hardware.

A footprint that “almost fits” may create poor pole geometry or exposed edges.

A generic sheet cut to the right dimensions may be better than a mismatched proprietary footprint.

## Does the Waterproof Side Need Protection?

Avoid dragging coated fabric across gravel.

Even if the coating is designed to face the ground, abrasion eventually wears material.

Lift and shake rather than pulling the footprint from beneath a loaded tent.

## Clean and Dry the Footprint

After a trip, shake dirt, rinse if needed, dry both sides, inspect holes, and store dry.

Footprints are often packed wet because they touch the ground.

Do not fold a wet footprint against a dry tent for long-term storage.

## How to Mark the Correct Side

Once you confirm orientation, make future setup easier.

Write “UP” on a tag, add a tiny colored cord to one corner, or save a setup photo.

Avoid unknown inks directly on coated fabric.

## Generic Tarp: Which Side Up?

For many ordinary woven polyethylene tarps, either face can function as a groundsheet.

Inspect for damage, grommet protrusions, dirt, or a different coating.

The critical setup is size and drainage.

## Scenario: Footprint With Four Identical Corners

If the fabric and hardware truly appear symmetrical, check the product page and manual. If no side is specified, orientation may not be functionally important.

Do not overthink a feature the manufacturer does not distinguish.

## Scenario: One Side Feels Rubbery

That may be the coated side.

Do not guess based only on feel. Confirm the model documentation because some coatings are intended to face the tent, while others are protected differently.

## Scenario: Fast-Pitch Setup

The answer becomes clear because the footprint must connect to pole ends and fly buckles in a specific orientation.

Set it up at home once, mark the front, and keep a photo.

## Common Myths

**“Shiny always goes down.”** Not universal.  
**“Logo always faces up.”** Often intuitive, not guaranteed.  
**“Footprints stop all leaks.”** They protect floors; they do not repair failed coatings.  
**“Bigger is better.”** Under a tent, oversized is worse in rain.

## Fast Decision Tree

**Model-specific footprint?** Check manual and attachments.  
**One side clearly coated?** Use manufacturer guidance.  
**Hardware only works one way?** Follow hardware.  
**Generic symmetrical tarp?** Either side is generally fine; size it correctly.  
**No information?** Prioritize fit and drainage and contact the manufacturer if needed.

## How Different Footprint Designs Signal Orientation

Some footprints use color-coded webbing that matches the tent corners. Others use buckles that only connect to the fly in one direction. Some have printed branding on one face but no functional difference.

Look for **functional cues before cosmetic cues**.

If a buckle will not align without twisting the footprint, you likely have the orientation wrong.

## Why Instructions May Say “Coated Side Up”

When the waterproof coating faces the tent floor, the more abrasion-resistant fabric face contacts the ground.

This can protect the coating.

But do not generalize to every product; construction varies by manufacturer.

## What If You Cannot Find the Manual?

Search the exact model name plus “footprint instructions.” Check the manufacturer's support archive.

If the product is discontinued, retailer PDFs and web archives may help.

When no documentation exists, fit the footprint by shape and attachment hardware and keep all edges under the floor.

## Marking Front and Back

Front/back confusion is often a bigger problem than up/down.

Add a small removable tag to the front-left loop once you confirm setup. On a dark rainy arrival, that saves time.

If the tent has two different doors, the footprint may appear nearly symmetrical while one end actually differs by a few inches.

## Does Orientation Affect Durability?

Potentially.

If one face is intended as the ground-contact surface, reversing it can expose a softer coating to rocks and grit.

That is why manufacturer guidance matters even when both sides seem waterproof.

## After-Trip Footprint Inspection

Hold the dry footprint toward bright light and look for pinholes.

A small hole in the footprint is not an emergency because the tent floor remains the main barrier, but repair it to preserve the protective layer.

Replace a footprint that has become badly shredded or has sharp damaged hardware.

## Creating Your Own Orientation Markers

Once you know the correct side and front, create a system that survives dark arrivals. A short piece of colored cord on the front-right corner is easy to feel with gloves. A small tag marked “UP” can remove uncertainty when both fabric faces look identical.

Keep the marking on webbing or a label rather than writing across waterproof coating.

If you lend the tent to someone else, include the same orientation cue in a setup note. A ten-second explanation prevents the footprint from being rotated, protruding, or connected to the wrong fly corners.

## The Practical Answer When You Are Already at Camp

If you cannot find instructions and rain is approaching, do not let footprint orientation delay shelter setup for half an hour. Match the footprint shape to the floor, align any usable attachment points, keep every edge underneath, and pitch the tent.

Once home, identify the exact model and confirm orientation before the next trip.

A correctly sized footprint on the “wrong” face for one night is generally a smaller problem than leaving the shelter unpitched in worsening weather. Hardware alignment and drainage are the immediate priorities.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the shiny side of a footprint go up?

Sometimes, but not as a universal rule. Follow the manufacturer instructions for the specific footprint.

### Can I use a footprint upside down?

It may still protect against abrasion, but model-specific coatings or fast-pitch hardware may work best only in the intended orientation.

### Should a footprint be visible outside the tent?

No. Keep it under the floor to avoid collecting rain.

### Can I use Tyvek or polycro instead?

Many campers use lightweight generic materials as groundsheets. Cut or fold them slightly smaller than the tent floor.

### Do footprints have a front and back?

Many model-specific footprints do because the tent floor is not perfectly symmetrical. Match corner hardware and shape.
