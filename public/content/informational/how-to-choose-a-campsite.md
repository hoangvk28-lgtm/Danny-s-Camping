# How to Choose a Campsite

Choosing a campsite looks simple until you arrive and realize that two sites in the same campground can feel completely different. One may stay dry, quiet, and shaded; another may collect water, sit next to a busy restroom path, or place your tent under questionable branches.

For most campers, the best campsite is not the one with the prettiest view. It is the one that matches your **shelter, vehicle, group, weather, and comfort priorities** while avoiding obvious hazards.

If you are still learning the basics, start with our guide on [how to go camping for the first time](/how-to-go-camping-for-the-first-time/). Once you know the type of trip you want, use the steps below to evaluate the actual site.

## Start With the Type of Campsite You Need

Before comparing individual sites, decide what category of campsite makes sense.

A developed campground may offer a numbered pad, parking space, picnic table, fire ring, toilet, and sometimes potable water. A primitive or dispersed location may provide few or none of those things.

Ask:

- Are you tent camping, sleeping in the vehicle, or bringing an RV?
- Do you need electrical hookups?
- How many tents and vehicles are in the group?
- Are children, older adults, or pets coming?
- Do you need easy restroom access?
- Will you cook elaborate meals?
- Are you sensitive to noise or bright campground lighting?

A site that works beautifully for a solo camper may be frustrating for a family of five.

If you are considering undeveloped public land, read [primitive camping vs dispersed camping](/primitive-camping-vs-dispersed-camping/) first because access and site-selection rules are often different.

## Check the Official Site Description Before Booking

Do not rely only on user photos.

Look for:

- Maximum vehicle length.
- Tent-pad dimensions.
- Number of vehicles allowed.
- Walk-in distance from parking.
- Shade level.
- Surface type.
- Accessibility.
- Water availability.
- Restroom location.
- Generator rules.
- Food-storage rules.
- Fire restrictions.
- Check-in and check-out times.

Some reservation systems include a site map. Use it.

A few minutes of map reading can tell you whether your site is beside the restroom, campground road, amphitheater, trash station, host site, river, or trailhead.

## Look for Level Ground

A slight slope may not look important in daylight. After several hours of sleeping on it, you may disagree.

For tent camping, choose ground that is:

- As level as practical.
- Large enough for the full tent footprint.
- Free of sharp rocks and roots.
- Not in an obvious drainage channel.
- Not lower than the surrounding ground.

When the ground slopes slightly, orient your head uphill rather than downhill.

For vehicle sleeping or RVs, leveling matters for comfort and, in some RV systems, appliance operation. Car campers may use approved leveling methods, while trailer and RV users often carry [leveling blocks](/best-rv-leveling-blocks/) or similar equipment.

## Think About Water Before It Rains

Many campsite mistakes are obvious only after the weather changes.

Avoid pitching in:

- Depressions.
- Dry-looking stream channels.
- Washes.
- Low spots below surrounding ground.
- Areas where runoff from roads or slopes will collect.

If rain is possible, study the terrain. Ask yourself, “If water starts moving downhill, where will it go?”

The National Park Service also advises campers to be aware of hazards such as flash flooding, wind, lightning, and dead branches when selecting a campsite.

## Look Up, Not Just Down

One of the easiest hazards to miss is overhead.

Before placing a tent, inspect nearby trees for:

- Dead limbs.
- Broken tops.
- Leaning dead trees.
- Hung-up branches.
- Obvious storm damage.

Do not camp under branches that appear unstable.

In high winds, even healthy trees can shed limbs. If a storm is expected, avoid exposed or questionable areas.

## Balance Shade and Sun

Shade is valuable in hot conditions, but full shade is not always ideal.

Morning sun can help:

- Dry condensation.
- Warm a cold campsite.
- Dry damp tents and chairs.

Afternoon shade can help:

- Keep the tent cooler.
- Reduce sun exposure.
- Make cooking and relaxing more pleasant.

In cold weather, a sunny site may be worth more than shade. In summer, western afternoon sun can turn a tent into an oven.

Choose based on season rather than always assuming “more shade is better.”

## Consider Wind Exposure

A scenic ridge can also be windy.

Look for natural shelter such as:

- Trees that are healthy and safely spaced.
- Terrain that blocks prevailing wind.
- Low vegetation outside sensitive areas.

Avoid setting up in the middle of an open field if storms are possible.

At the same time, do not hide your tent in dense brush simply to escape wind. You still need a legal, durable surface with enough airflow.

If strong winds are expected, pair careful site selection with proper staking. Our guide on [how to secure a tent in high winds](/how-to-secure-a-tent-in-high-winds/) covers that in more detail.

## Think About Restroom Distance

Being close to the restroom is convenient at 2 a.m.

It also can mean:

- More foot traffic.
- Doors opening and closing.
- Flashlights.
- Conversations.
- Odors.
- Early-morning activity.

Families with young children may happily accept those trade-offs. Light sleepers may prefer to be farther away.

There is no universal best distance.

## Watch the Campground Road

Roadside sites are easy to access, but they may be noisier and dustier.

Check whether the site sits near:

- Main entrance.
- Dump station.
- Water fill.
- Group camp.
- Boat launch.
- Trailhead.
- Camp host.

A quiet-looking campground map can become busy if your site is on the route everyone uses.

## Consider Food Storage Before You Unpack

In wildlife country, the food-storage setup should influence where and how you camp.

Some campgrounds provide lockers. Others permit specific vehicle storage. Some require approved bear-resistant containers.

Do not assume the cooler can sit next to the tent.

Read [how to keep food safe while camping](/how-to-keep-food-safe-while-camping/) and the local food-storage regulations before arrival.

## Developed Campsite Layout

Once you arrive at a numbered campground site, identify four zones:

1. **Sleeping area**
2. **Cooking/eating area**
3. **Food storage**
4. **Fire area**, if fires are allowed

Keep traffic paths clear.

Avoid placing tent guylines across the route to the picnic table or restroom.

Do not put the tent dangerously close to the fire ring. Sparks can damage or ignite synthetic tent fabric.

## Backcountry and Dispersed Site Selection

When you are outside developed campgrounds, your responsibilities increase.

Use durable surfaces and existing sites when local rules recommend them.

Avoid expanding campsites by:

- Cutting vegetation.
- Moving rocks unnecessarily.
- Building furniture.
- Creating new fire rings.
- Driving off legal routes.

Stay the required distance from water, roads, trails, and cultural resources. Requirements vary by location, so check the exact land-management unit.

If your goal is to save money, do not confuse “free” with “unregulated.” Our guide to [how to find free campsites](/how-to-find-free-campsites/) explains how to confirm that a site is actually legal.

## Campsite Choice for Families

Families usually benefit from:

- Flat space.
- Clear boundaries.
- Short restroom walks.
- Low vehicle traffic.
- Safe play area.
- Shade.
- Easy access to water.

Avoid steep drop-offs, fast water, and heavy traffic when young children are part of the group.

## Campsite Choice for Hot Weather

Prioritize:

- Afternoon shade.
- Airflow.
- Distance from heat-radiating rock or pavement.
- Safe access to water.
- Space for a shade shelter if allowed.

If temperatures are extreme, reconsider the trip rather than trying to solve dangerous heat with campsite tricks.

## Campsite Choice for Cold Weather

Prioritize:

- Wind protection.
- Morning sun.
- Ground that will stay dry.
- Safe access road.
- Space for a properly insulated sleep system.

Cold air can settle in low basins overnight, so the lowest possible spot is not always the warmest.

## Campsite Choice for Rain

Look for:

- Slightly elevated ground.
- Firm surface.
- Good drainage.
- Enough space to keep entry areas from becoming mud pits.
- Safe tarp setup if permitted.

Never dig trenches around your tent. Modern campsite ethics favor selecting a naturally suitable site instead of altering the ground.

## Campsite Choice for Privacy

Privacy often means accepting a longer walk to facilities.

Look for:

- Vegetation between sites.
- Sites at the end of loops.
- Fewer neighboring pads.
- Separation from group areas.

Do not create privacy by moving into closed or protected vegetation.

## Five-Minute Campsite Evaluation

When you arrive, do this before unloading:

**Minute 1:** Walk the site boundary.  
**Minute 2:** Look up for branch hazards.  
**Minute 3:** Identify the flattest sleeping area.  
**Minute 4:** Visualize rain runoff and traffic paths.  
**Minute 5:** Decide where cooking, food storage, and seating will go.

Only then unload.

That five-minute pause prevents a surprising amount of rework.

## Common Campsite Selection Mistakes

### Choosing only for the view
Views matter less after dark than wind, drainage, and level ground.

### Setting up immediately
Walking the entire site first often reveals a much better tent position.

### Ignoring campground traffic
Restrooms and roads can be much busier than expected.

### Camping in low spots
A comfortable flat depression can become a puddle.

### Forgetting overhead hazards
A perfect tent pad under dead branches is not a good campsite.

### Assuming every site has the same rules
Food storage, fires, generators, and parking can vary.

## Best Campsite Checklist

Choose a site that is:

- Legal.
- Large enough.
- Level.
- Well drained.
- Free of obvious overhead hazards.
- Appropriate for expected weather.
- Compatible with your vehicle.
- Convenient enough for your group.
- Set up to meet food-storage rules.
- On durable ground.

## Campsite Scenarios: What Would I Choose?

### Scenario 1: Family with young children
I would usually prioritize a relatively flat site with clear boundaries, moderate shade, low vehicle traffic, and reasonable restroom access. I would accept a little more campground activity in exchange for convenience. A site at the end of a loop can be attractive if it reduces through traffic.

### Scenario 2: Couple seeking quiet
I would look farther from restrooms, dumpsters, playgrounds, and group sites. An outer-loop site with vegetation between neighbors can feel more private, provided it is still safe and level.

### Scenario 3: Hot summer weekend
Shade and airflow move near the top of the list. I would avoid an exposed west-facing site with no tree cover, especially if the tent will sit in afternoon sun. Water access also becomes more important.

### Scenario 4: Rainy forecast
Drainage becomes the deciding factor. A pretty site at the bottom of a slope is a bad trade. I would choose slightly elevated, firm ground and make sure the tent entrance is not facing the direction runoff will travel.

### Scenario 5: Windy forecast
I would avoid open ridgelines, isolated exposed clearings, and questionable overhead branches. A lower, sheltered site can be less scenic but much more comfortable.

## Questions to Ask When Comparing Two Campsites

If two sites look similar on a reservation map, compare them with these questions:

- Which one has the better tent pad?
- Which one has less traffic?
- Which one has better afternoon shade?
- Which one is safer if rain arrives?
- Which one is easier for the least-mobile person in the group?
- Which one has enough room for our actual tent, not just the stated occupancy?
- Which one makes food storage simpler?
- Which one gives us a cleaner path between tent, table, and vehicle?

Do not overvalue waterfront or cliff-edge scenery if those features also add wind, insects, traffic, or safety concerns.

## How to Read Campsite Photos More Critically

Reservation photos can be useful, but they are often taken in flattering conditions.

Look beyond the center of the image.

Check:

- Tree cover and sun direction.
- Slope behind the site.
- Neighbor distance.
- Road proximity.
- Parking angle.
- Tent-pad surface.
- Whether the picnic table occupies most of the usable space.

If user reviews repeatedly mention mud, noise, or uneven tent pads, treat that as a signal to investigate further rather than dismissing one bad review.

## FAQ

### Is it better to camp near water?
Not automatically. Water can improve scenery and recreation, but it may also bring insects, humidity, flooding risk, cold air, and additional regulations. Follow required setback distances and choose water access deliberately.

### Should a tent face east?
Some campers enjoy morning sun, but there is no universal best direction. Wind, rain, terrain, privacy, and entry convenience matter more than compass direction alone.

### Is a gravel tent pad good?
Gravel can drain well but may feel firm under thin sleeping pads. Use the tent only on surfaces allowed by the campground and make sure stakes are compatible with the pad.

### How close should I camp to the bathroom?
Close enough for your group to use it comfortably, but far enough that lights, doors, odors, and traffic do not disturb you. The “best” distance depends on your group.

### What if the assigned campsite looks unsafe?
Do not ignore a real hazard. Contact campground staff or the land manager if possible. If there is an immediate safety issue such as dangerous branches, flooding, or severe weather exposure, choose safety over convenience and follow staff instructions.

## DannyCamping Rule of Thumb

When choosing between a beautiful site and an easy site, beginners are usually happier with the easy site.

Flat ground, predictable weather protection, safe trees, and a practical layout will improve every hour you spend at camp. The view is a bonus.

## Final Takeaway

A good campsite removes problems before they begin.

Look beyond the scenery. Evaluate the ground, drainage, trees, wind, sun, facilities, food-storage requirements, and campground traffic.

Then use [how to set up a campsite](/how-to-set-up-a-campsite/) to turn that location into an efficient place to sleep, cook, relax, and pack up.
