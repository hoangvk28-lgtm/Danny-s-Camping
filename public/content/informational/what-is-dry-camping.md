# What Is Dry Camping?

Dry camping means staying at a campsite **without utility hookups**.

For RVers and vehicle campers, that usually means no:

- Fresh-water hookup.
- Sewer hookup.
- Electrical shore power.

You rely on what you bring with you: onboard water, batteries, propane or another cooking fuel, and your own waste-storage system.

Dry camping can happen in a developed campground, overflow area, festival, parking area where overnight use is legal, or remote public land.

The term describes the lack of hookups, not the location.

## Dry Camping vs Boondocking

These terms overlap, but they are not identical.

**Dry camping** = camping without utility hookups.

**Boondocking** = commonly used for self-contained camping away from developed campgrounds, often on public land or in other undeveloped locations.

So you can dry camp in a formal campground that has numbered sites but no hookups.

You can also boondock in a remote area, which is almost always a form of dry camping.

## Dry Camping vs Dispersed Camping

Dispersed camping usually refers to camping outside a developed campground.

Dry camping refers to utilities.

Examples:

- A national forest dispersed site with no services: both dispersed and dry camping.
- A campground with toilets and paved sites but no hookups: dry camping, but not dispersed.
- A full-hookup RV park: neither.

## What Do You Need for Dry Camping?

You need systems for:

1. Water.
2. Power.
3. Waste.
4. Food.
5. Temperature control.
6. Lighting.
7. Communication.

How complicated those systems need to be depends on your vehicle and trip length.

## Water

Without a water hookup, you need to bring enough water or know where you can legally refill.

Water is used for:

- Drinking.
- Cooking.
- Handwashing.
- Dishwashing.
- Toilet flushing in some RVs.
- Pets.

Track consumption rather than guessing.

A short trip may be easy with onboard water plus bottled drinking water.

Long trips require much more careful management.

## Power

Without shore power, common options include:

- House battery.
- Portable power station.
- Solar panels.
- Generator where legal and appropriate.
- Vehicle charging.

Your needs depend on what you run.

Low-power camping may only require:

- Lights.
- Phones.
- Small fan.
- Water pump.

High-power setups may add:

- Refrigerator.
- Laptop.
- CPAP.
- Induction cooking.
- Microwave.
- Air conditioner.

Large appliances can dramatically change the battery and charging system required.

## Solar

Solar can extend dry-camping time, but it is not unlimited power.

Output depends on:

- Panel wattage.
- Sun angle.
- Shade.
- Clouds.
- Season.
- Battery capacity.
- Charge-controller limits.

A solar panel does not replace energy planning.

You still need to know how much electricity you use each day.

## Generator

A generator can provide substantial power but introduces:

- Fuel.
- Noise.
- Maintenance.
- Exhaust.
- Campground-hour restrictions.
- Fire restrictions.

Never operate a fuel-burning generator inside a vehicle, tent, or enclosed occupied space because of carbon-monoxide danger.

Follow manufacturer instructions and local regulations.

## Waste

RV dry camping requires managing:

- Gray water.
- Black water.
- Trash.

Do not dump wastewater unless it is legal at an approved location.

Know the capacity of your tanks and plan where you will empty them.

For tent or vehicle campers without an RV toilet system, human-waste rules depend on the destination.

## Food and Refrigeration

You can use:

- Cooler.
- 12V refrigerator.
- Propane refrigerator in compatible RV systems.
- Shelf-stable foods.

Powered refrigeration increases electrical demand.

A cooler uses no electricity but requires ice management.

Choose based on trip length and your power system.

## How Long Can You Dry Camp?

The limiting resource is different for every setup.

It may be:

- Fresh water.
- Battery capacity.
- Waste tank capacity.
- Food.
- Propane.
- Legal stay limit.

A large battery does not help if you run out of water.

A huge water tank does not help if the waste tank fills first.

Track all systems.

## Simple Dry-Camping Calculation

Imagine your setup has:

- 30 gallons of usable fresh water.
- Battery capacity sufficient for three days.
- Waste capacity for four days.

If your group uses ten gallons of water per day, your realistic limit may be about three days even though other systems last longer.

The shortest resource determines the trip length.

## How to Conserve Water

Common strategies include:

- Use a low-flow faucet.
- Turn water off while soaping dishes.
- Pre-clean pans with a scraper or paper towel.
- Cook simple meals.
- Use campground toilets when available and appropriate.
- Take shorter showers.

Do not compromise hygiene or hydration simply to extend a trip.

## How to Conserve Power

Start with the largest loads.

Reduce unnecessary use of:

- Electric heating.
- Air conditioning.
- Electric cooking.
- High-powered appliances.

LED lighting, phones, and small electronics use much less energy than heating or cooling equipment.

## Is Dry Camping Only for RVs?

No.

Tent campers can also dry camp in the general sense of camping without utility services.

But the term is most common in RV, van, trailer, and overlanding discussions because hookups are a major part of campground planning for those vehicles.

## Beginner Dry-Camping Setup

For a short vehicle-based trip, a simple setup might include:

- Drinking water.
- Larger water jug.
- Cooler or 12V fridge.
- Small power station.
- Solar panel if useful.
- Headlamps.
- Camp stove.
- Trash storage.
- Legal toilet or waste plan.

Start with one or two nights so you can measure real water and power consumption.

## Common Dry-Camping Mistakes

### Bringing too little water
People often track drinking water but forget cooking and cleaning.

### Overestimating solar
Shade or clouds can cut production.

### Running high-power appliances casually
Heating and cooling loads can drain batteries quickly.

### Ignoring waste capacity
Waste can become the limiting factor.

### Assuming overnight parking equals legal camping
Rules vary by land owner and municipality.

### Depending on cell service
Download maps and important information before arriving.

## Who Is Dry Camping Good For?

Dry camping is useful for campers who want:

- More campsite options.
- Less dependence on full-service RV parks.
- Access to basic public campgrounds.
- Greater self-sufficiency.
- Short stays away from developed utilities.

It does require planning.

## Final Takeaway

Dry camping is simply camping without utility hookups.

To do it comfortably, manage water, power, waste, food, and legal camping access as connected systems.

For beginners, start with a short trip. Measure how much water and electricity you actually use. Those real numbers will tell you more than any generic rule about how long your setup can stay off-grid.
